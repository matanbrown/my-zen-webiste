Alan Watts (1915–1973) was born in England and died in California, and
in between managed to do something almost no one before him had
managed quite so well: take complex Eastern ideas — Zen, Taoism,
Buddhism — and make them accessible to a wide Western audience without
watering them down into empty slogans. He did it mainly through
lectures, radio programs, and dozens of books written in language that
was clear, sharp, and often genuinely funny — a rare combination when
you're talking about things like "emptiness" or "the self as illusion."

In 1936, at 21, he met D.T. Suzuki for the first time, one of the most
influential Western scholars of Zen, and absorbed the fundamentals of
Buddhist philosophy through his writings. A few years later, after
moving to New York, he trained briefly under the teacher Sokei-an
Sasaki, a Zen roshi who founded the first Zen institute in America —
but he left the formal training because the traditional teaching
method didn't suit him. That's a detail worth keeping in mind: Watts
was never a formally certified Zen teacher in the traditional sense,
and he knew that about himself perfectly well.

Which is exactly why one of his more interesting essays is "Beat Zen,
Square Zen, and Zen," from 1958 — a text in which he sharply criticizes
both ways the West had "adopted" Zen: on one hand as an unruly fashion
among the Beat generation, on the other as a rigid, disconnected ritual
in Western dharma centers. Watts didn't spare himself or the audience
he'd helped create — a kind of intellectual honesty that isn't a given.
In the 1960s he was among the figures who helped establish the Esalen
Institute in California, which became a center of the "human potential
movement" — and from there his influence spilled well past the borders
of Buddhism, into psychology, art, and the counterculture of the era.

The central idea that runs through nearly everything he wrote is that
our sense of a separate "self," wrapped in skin, disconnected from the
world, is an illusion. And as should be clear by now, Watts wasn't a
flawless figure: he struggled with alcoholism and a turbulent personal
life, and never presented himself as a saint. Maybe that's exactly why
his writing doesn't read like preaching from above — it reads like
someone genuinely trying to understand, just like the rest of us.

Of the many books he wrote, *The Way of Zen* (1957) is the best
recommendation for someone just starting out — a clear, comprehensive
introduction to Zen, to its roots in Taoism and Buddhism, and to its
expression in art and everyday life in the East. You can find it
[in English](https://www.amazon.com/Way-Zen-Alan-W-Watts/dp/0679723013)
or [in Hebrew](https://e-vrit.co.il/product/6875/%D7%93%D7%A8%D7%9A-%D7%94%D7%96%D7%9F).

## How I got to know him

My introduction to Alan Watts began as part of my entry into the world
of Zen, through the teachings of my teacher, Yuval Ido Tal. One of the
ideas I find most interesting about Alan Watts is the fact that we look
at the world through tools that only show us part of reality, and we
get confused and think that's reality itself. We know, for example,
that animals experience reality differently — they might have a more
developed sense of smell, or the ability to see at night — but we
assume that what we experience as reality is something objective, the
truth. Another example is using a microscope to look at things, and
suddenly we see a completely different world.

So what this actually means is that we perceive reality through the
tools we've been given — sight, hearing, touch, and so on — but we
forget that this is only part of reality, not reality itself. And
that's exactly the thread connecting Watts's "self as illusion" to
sitting practice itself — the moment you stop confusing the tool with
what it shows you, what's left is just what's there.
