Alan Watts was born in 1915 in England and died in 1973. He was one of
the central figures who brought Eastern philosophy — Buddhism, Zen, and
Taoism — to a wide Western audience, mainly through lectures, radio
programs, and books written in unusually clear, accessible language,
even when the ideas themselves were abstract.

In 1936, at 21, he met D.T. Suzuki for the first time, one of the most
influential Western scholars of Zen, and absorbed the basic concepts of
Buddhist philosophy through his writings. A few years later, after
moving to New York, he trained briefly under the teacher Sokei-an
Sasaki, a Zen roshi who founded the first Zen institute in America — but
he left the formal training because the traditional teaching method
didn't suit him.

Of the many books he wrote, *The Way of Zen* (1957) is the best
recommendation for someone just starting out — a clear, comprehensive
introduction to Zen, to its roots in Taoism and Buddhism, and to its
expression in art and everyday life in the East. You can find it
[in English](https://www.amazon.com/Way-Zen-Alan-W-Watts/dp/0679723013)
or [in Hebrew](https://e-vrit.co.il/product/6875/%D7%93%D7%A8%D7%9A-%D7%94%D7%96%D7%9F).

## How I got to know him

My introduction to Alan Watts began as part of my entry into the world
of Zen, through the teachings of my teacher, Yuval Ido Tal. One of the
ideas I find most interesting about Alan Watts is the fact that we look
at the world through tools that only show us part of reality, and we
get confused and think that's reality itself. We know, for example,
that animals experience reality differently — they might have a more
developed sense of smell, or the ability to see at night — but we
assume that what we experience as reality is something objective, the
truth. Another example is using a microscope to look at things, and
suddenly we see a completely different world.

So what this actually means is that we perceive reality through the
tools we've been given — sight, hearing, touch, and so on — but we
forget that this is only part of reality, not reality itself.
