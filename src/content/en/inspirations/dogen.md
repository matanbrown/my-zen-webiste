Dogen Zenji (道元禅師, 1200–1253) is the founder of the Soto school of
Zen in Japan — the same school this site's own practice belongs to. He
was born in Kyoto to an aristocratic family, orphaned by both parents
in childhood (his mother died when he was seven or eight), and was
deeply struck by the impermanence of existence. At thirteen he climbed
Mount Hiei and was ordained as a monk in the Tendai school — the
dominant Buddhist tradition in Japan at the time, heavy with ritual,
politics, and status.

His dissatisfaction with Tendai, and above all a question that had
followed him since childhood — if all beings are already endowed with
Buddha-nature, what's the point of practice at all? — led him to look
for answers beyond Japan. In 1223, at twenty-three, he sailed to China
with his teacher Myozen (a disciple of Eisai, who had brought Rinzai
Zen to Japan). There, after a period of searching, he found his true
teacher: Rujing, a monk of the Chinese Caodong school — the Chinese
counterpart to Soto. Under Rujing's guidance Dogen underwent what's
described as a defining awakening — "casting off body and mind"
(shinjin datsuraku) — and received Dharma transmission from him.

## The shift he brought

When he returned to Japan in 1227, Dogen didn't bring back another
meditation technique — he brought a conceptual reversal. Until then,
both in Zen and in other schools, practice tended to be understood as
a means — a path that eventually leads to a separate destination called
"enlightenment." Dogen argued the opposite: zazen isn't a means to
attaining enlightenment, it's **the expression itself** of enlightenment
already present. "Practice and enlightenment are one" (修証一等,
shushō-ittō) — not two stages in sequence, but the same thing. Out of
this came the practice he's most identified with: shikantaza — "just
sitting" — sitting with no external goal to achieve, no meditation
object, no expectation of a breakthrough. The sitting itself is the
point.

In 1243, after friction with the Buddhist establishment in Kyoto, Dogen
moved to the remote mountains of Echizen (today's Fukui Prefecture),
where he founded Eihei-ji — a monastery that remains, to this day, one
of the two head temples of the Soto school. There he also wrote most of
the *Shobogenzo* (正法眼蔵, "Treasury of the True Dharma Eye"), his
monumental collection of essays — one of the deepest and most
challenging texts in all of Zen literature.

Dogen died in Kyoto in 1253, after naming his disciple Koun Ejo as his
successor. The school he founded later spread mainly through his
disciple's disciple, Keizan Jokin, until it became one of the largest
branches of Japanese Buddhism — around 14,000 temples still operating
under its name today.
