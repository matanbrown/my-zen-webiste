You've probably never heard of Antaiji. I hadn't either, until I started
looking for a Zen monastery in Japan that lets practitioners from outside
Japan join its retreats.

I spent five nights there, and the point of this post is simply to give
a clear picture of what life at the monastery actually looks like. How
you get there, what you do during the day, what you eat, what surprised
me, and who I think this experience could be a good fit for.

## Why Antaiji specifically?

There are quite a few Zen monasteries in Japan, but Antaiji is one of
the few places that runs retreats specifically for practitioners from
outside Japan. That was one of the main reasons I chose to go there.

If you're looking to experience an active Zen monastery in the country
where Soto Zen originated, in my view this is an excellent option.

## How do you get there?

I came from Kyoto.

The trip involves about four hours by train to Hamasaka station, then a
bus toward Tottori, and finally about an hour's walk to the monastery.

Antaiji's website has detailed, up-to-date directions, so I'd recommend
using those before you travel.

## How much does it cost?

The retreat I attended cost 55,000 yen for five nights.

The price may change, so it's always worth checking the current price
on Antaiji's official site.

## What does the daily schedule look like?

The daily schedule is fixed and simple.

The day is made up of meditation sittings, meals, cleaning, physical
work at the monastery, and a short rest period.

When you're not sitting in meditation, you work — mostly in the fields
or the kitchen, depending on what's needed that particular day.

## The food

The food is very simple.

Most meals were rice, miso soup, and a few small side dishes.

One thing I especially loved was that most of the food comes from what
the monastery grows itself. There's a real sense of connection between
the work in the fields and what ends up on your plate afterward.

## What's worth bringing?

I'd recommend bringing:

- Comfortable clothes for meditation.
- Clothes you don't mind getting dirty doing fieldwork.
- A hat for working in the sun.
- Comfortable shoes.

Beyond that, you don't need much.

## What surprised me?

The gentleness, actually.

Before I arrived I imagined a very rigid monastery with lots of rules
and regulations.

In practice I felt an atmosphere far more human than I'd expected.
There's a clear structure, but the way people treated each other was
kind, patient, and not rigid at all.

## What was hardest for me?

The long meditation sittings, without a doubt.

I actually wrote a separate piece about that part, because for me it
was one of the most significant experiences of the retreat. If that
interests you, you're welcome to read it: [The Question That Stayed With Me — Pain During Meditation](/en/lessons/2026-08-03-the-question-that-stayed/)

## The moments that stayed with me

Looking back, these are the moments I remember most vividly:

- The work in the fields.
- The landscape surrounding the monastery.
- The ceremonies.
- And the simplicity of life for a few days.

## Would I recommend going?

If you're curious to experience what life at a Zen monastery in Japan
looks like, I'd absolutely recommend it.

You don't need much meditation experience going in, but it does help to
arrive with openness, a willingness to do physical work, and a lot of
curiosity.

For all the current information on retreats, registration dates, and
how to get there, I'd recommend visiting Antaiji's official site:

[Antaiji's official site](https://www.antaiji.org/en/)
