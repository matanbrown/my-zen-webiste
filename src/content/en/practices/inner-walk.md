Inner Walk is a walking meditation practice developed on Koh Phangan,
Thailand, by Phra Olarn, a Buddhist monk born on the island, who
passed away in 2024.

At the center of the practice is a very simple act: walking.

You don't try to stop your thoughts, reach quiet, or produce some
particular state of mind. Walking creates a frame within which you
can start noticing what's already happening — thoughts, emotions,
bodily sensations, and our reactions to them.

The practice draws inspiration from Buddhism, Taoism, and Zen, but
doesn't try to teach them as a philosophical system. The ideas are
conveyed in simple language and through images that are easy to
carry afterward into life outside the practice too.

One of the central ideas in Inner Walk is that we spend a large part
of our time inside thoughts, stories, expectations, and memories, and
often barely notice it. Walking isn't meant to make them disappear.
It lets you see them as they appear, and return again and again to
what's happening now.

On Koh Phangan there's a four-day process. Participants walk in
silence for a few hours each day, alongside talks and explanations
about the practice and sharing circles. Participation is
donation-based.

## How it actually works

You walk, again and again, along the same short route.

And that's more or less everything you'd see from the outside.

But while walking, something starts to reveal itself.

A thought appears. Another follows it. Maybe impatience shows up.
Maybe boredom. Maybe the body wants to stop. Sometimes a memory
arrives, sometimes a strong emotion, and sometimes there are a few
moments where you're simply walking.

There's no need to choose what appears. The practice is learning to
recognize all of it as it's happening.

You can think of this as training an "awareness muscle": the more we
practice recognizing a thought as a thought, an emotion as an
emotion, and a reaction as a reaction, sometimes a little more space
opens up before we act out of them automatically.

In this sense, walking isn't only something you do during the
retreat. It serves as a kind of simple laboratory for observing the
mind — because the same things that show up while walking —
impatience, wanting something to end, judgment, expectation, fear, or
pleasure — also show up in the rest of life. It's just that while
walking, there's less room to run from them.

## Is this Buddhist walking meditation?

Yes and no.

Walking meditation has a long history across different Buddhist
traditions, but Inner Walk is a modern practice that Phra Olarn
developed out of various sources and influences.

In certain Buddhist walking meditations, attention is deliberately
directed toward the movement of the body, the contact of the feet
with the ground, or another meditation object. In Inner Walk, walking
serves as a more open frame for inquiry into whatever is arising in
the mind.

So I find it useful to see it as a practice that stands on its own,
rather than trying to fully identify it with vipassana, Zen, or
another traditional meditation method.

## How I got to know it

After meeting Yuval "Fluke" Birman
I started practicing Inner Walk.

One of the things that struck me right away was how little you
actually have to do.

Physically, walking was simpler and more natural for me than sitting
for long stretches. But very quickly I discovered that through this
simple act I meet a lot of the same things I meet in sitting:
thoughts that keep arriving, restlessness, emotions, the wish for
something to change — and sometimes moments where there's nothing
that needs to be done with any of it.

There's something in Inner Walk that reminds me of shikantaza in
motion. Not because the two practices are the same — they come from
different frameworks and have different histories and instructions —
but because of something in the approach.

I don't need to try to reach a particular state.

If there are thoughts, there are thoughts.

If there's restlessness, there's restlessness.

And if, for a moment, you're simply walking — you're simply walking.

What speaks to me in this practice is that simplicity: trying less to
manage whatever shows up, and learning more to see it. And then
continuing to walk.
