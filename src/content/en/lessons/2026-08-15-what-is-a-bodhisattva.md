The word Bodhisattva is made up of the Sanskrit words Bodhi —
awakening, and Sattva — a being. It can simply be understood as "one
who turns toward awakening" or "a being aspiring to awakening."

But to understand what a bodhisattva is, it helps to distinguish
between the term's early use and the central place it later took on
in Mahayana Buddhism.

In the early Buddhist texts, the Pali equivalent, Bodhisatta, appears
mainly when the Buddha refers to himself before he became a buddha.
The Jataka tales describe his previous lives, and the long path along
which he cultivated qualities that eventually ripened into awakening.

Mahayana, then, didn't invent the bodhisattva. It did something else,
and something very significant: it turned the bodhisattva path into a
central ideal of practice. Not just the story of someone who would
one day become a buddha, but a path the practitioner themselves is
invited to enter.

## The Bodhisattva Path

At the heart of the path is Bodhicitta — the aspiration to awaken for
the benefit of all beings.

This isn't only a decision to be a better person. Bodhicitta joins
two things Mahayana refuses to separate: wisdom and compassion.

Wisdom sees that things don't exist as independent, fixed entities,
and that the "I" we're used to protecting isn't separate from the
conditions and relationships within which it exists. Compassion meets
the suffering found within this world.

So the bodhisattva path isn't a choice between working on yourself
and helping others. It starts to question that very separation.

## The Six Perfections

Mahayana literature often describes the bodhisattva path through the
six paramitas — qualities, or "perfections," the bodhisattva
cultivates:

**Generosity — Dāna.** Giving without turning the giving into a
transaction.

**Ethical conduct — Śīla.** Living and acting in a way that reduces
harm.

**Patience — Kṣānti.** The capacity to meet difficulty, pain, and
people who don't behave the way we'd want, without reacting
immediately out of aversion or rejection.

**Diligence — Vīrya.** Continuing on the path even after the initial
enthusiasm fades.

**Meditation — Dhyāna.** Cultivating stability, presence, and
gathering of the mind.

**Wisdom — Prajñā.** Seeing [impermanence, not-self](/en/lessons/2026-08-15-the-three-marks-of-existence/), and emptiness in
a way that doesn't stay only a philosophical idea.

The paramitas aren't rungs completed one after another. They develop
together. Generosity without wisdom can turn into trying to save
others in order to feel good about ourselves. Wisdom without
compassion can turn into detachment. Patience without discernment can
turn into passively accepting harm.

The bodhisattva path is the ongoing practice of all of these within
life itself.

## The Four Bodhisattva Vows

In Zen traditions, the four bodhisattva vows are recited again and
again. The exact wording varies slightly between traditions and
translations, but the spirit is:

*Beings are numberless — I vow to free them all.*
*Delusions are inexhaustible — I vow to end them all.*
*Dharma gates are boundless — I vow to enter them all.*
*The Buddha way is unattainable — I vow to attain it.*

There's something almost absurd about these vows. Each one is
impossible. If beings are numberless, you can't reach the last one.
If delusions are inexhaustible, you can't mark a day when they've all
ended. If the dharma gates are infinite, you can't pass through all
of them. And if the Buddha way is boundless, there's no point at
which you can say: I've arrived.

But the vows aren't a work plan we're expected to complete. They
orient the practice. We vow to turn toward all beings precisely
because there's no end to the beings we can meet. We vow to meet
delusions precisely because they keep recurring.

The vow doesn't say "One day I'll finish." It says: "This is the
direction I'm turning my life toward."

## Does the bodhisattva give up nirvana?

In the West, a description sometimes recurs of the bodhisattva as
someone who has reached the gates of nirvana but refuses to enter
until every other being enters first.

That image captures the spirit of bodhisattva compassion well, but as
an explanation of Mahayana it's partial and can mislead.

The bodhisattva isn't simply someone who could be liberated but
decides to keep suffering a while longer in order to help others. In
Mahayana thought, especially in light of the teaching of emptiness,
the very distinction between "my liberation" and a world of beings
who remain "outside" it becomes less straightforward.

If there's no independent, separate self, awakening isn't private
property that a person acquires for themselves either. Compassion,
then, isn't a good deed the awakened person decides to perform after
awakening. Compassion is an expression of the awakening itself.

## Emptiness and Compassion

This is one of the most important connections in the bodhisattva
path.

You might think emptiness leads to the conclusion: if there's no
fixed self and everything is empty, then nothing really matters.

Mahayana points almost in the opposite direction. Because nothing
exists on its own, everything exists in relation to other things. My
life isn't a closed unit. The food I eat, the air I breathe, the
language I think in, the body I was given, the people who taught me,
and the society I live within — all of these are part of the
conditions that make possible what I call "I."

Seeing emptiness isn't meant to distance us from the world. When the
rigid separation between "self" and "other" softens, the suffering of
the other also becomes harder to ignore.

Hence the deep connection in Mahayana between Prajñā — wisdom, and
Karuṇā — compassion.

## The Great Bodhisattvas

Mahayana sutras and traditions also feature figures of great
bodhisattvas, each especially identified with a particular aspect of
the path.

**Avalokiteśvara** embodies compassion. In China he's known as
Guanyin, and in Japan as Kannon. The Heart Sutra, recited daily in
many Zen monasteries, opens precisely with him.

**Mañjuśrī** embodies wisdom. In Buddhist art he's sometimes depicted
with a sword cutting through ignorance and delusion.

**Kṣitigarbha**, known in Japan as Jizo, is identified with a vow to
reach even the most difficult places of suffering. In Japan he's also
especially associated with protecting children and the dead.

**Samantabhadra** is identified with practice, action, and the
bodhisattva vows.

For some Buddhists, these are real, awakened beings one can turn to
in prayer and practice. Others meet them also as expressions of
qualities that can be discovered and cultivated within ourselves.
There's no need to rush to decide between these two ways of reading
them in order to understand their place in the tradition.

## And what does this have to do with Zen?

Zen is Mahayana Buddhism. So the bodhisattva path isn't a side idea
added onto meditation. It's part of the frame within which meditation
takes on meaning.

It's very easy to think of zazen as a private matter: I sit. I
observe my mind. I want to be less anxious, more calm, maybe even to
awaken.

But the bodhisattva path asks what happens once the sitting ends. How
do I speak with the person in front of me? How do I eat? How do I
work? What do I do when someone makes me angry? How do I respond to
suffering I'd rather not see? How do I use my time, my body, and my
attention?

In Zen, and especially in [the Soto tradition](/en/lessons/2026-08-15-buddhism-is-not-one-thing/), everyday activities
aren't just breaks between periods of meditation. Cooking, cleaning,
eating, working, and caring for others can all be an expression of
practice.

If our sitting doesn't gradually find expression in how we meet the
world, it's at least worth asking what exactly we're practicing.

## Not turning the bodhisattva into a new identity

There's another trap here.

You can learn about the bodhisattva and think: from now on I have to
be compassionate. I have to be generous. Patient. Calm. Never angry.
Always put others first.

And then the bodhisattva path turns into just another ideal of "who
I'm supposed to be." That's just another form of grasping at self.

Compassion doesn't require us to say yes to everything. Patience
doesn't require staying somewhere that's harming us. Not-self doesn't
mean we have no boundaries.

Sometimes bodhisattva action is giving. Sometimes it's saying no.
Sometimes it's speaking. And sometimes it's staying silent.

The question isn't "What is a compassionate person supposed to do?"
but "What does this moment ask of me, when I'm not acting only out of
my own grasping?"

There's no formula that answers this every time. That's exactly why
it's a path of practice.

## The bodhisattva sitting right here

It's easy to think of a bodhisattva as a distant figure — a statue in
a temple, an awakened being from the sutras, or someone who devoted
decades to practice.

But the bodhisattva vow brings the question back to somewhere much
closer. To the person in front of us. To the plate we're washing. To
the anger arising right now. To the moment we can choose to listen or
not to listen. To the small possibility of acting differently.

There's no need to wait until we're wise enough or calm enough to
begin the bodhisattva path. Even the wish to be a "good bodhisattva"
is something that can be set down for a moment.

There's only this encounter. And then the next one.

## A vow with no end

The bodhisattva path doesn't promise we'll reach a point where we no
longer get angry, no longer make mistakes, and no longer hurt anyone.

The vows themselves remind us that the path is larger than our
capacity to complete it.

We'll make mistakes. We'll forget. We'll act out of fear or out of
habit.

And then we'll see it. We'll come back. And we'll try again.

Not because one day we'll manage to finish the bodhisattva path. But
because the returning itself is the path.

*Beings are numberless — I vow to free them all.*

And then you meet the being standing in front of you right now.
