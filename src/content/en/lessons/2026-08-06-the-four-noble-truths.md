In his first teaching, at the Deer Park in Sarnath, the Buddha didn't
open with a story about himself, and he didn't open with a system of
belief. He opened with a description.

Four observations, known together as the Four Noble Truths. Not
presented as abstract theory, but as something he invited his
listeners to check for themselves, against their own experience.

**The first truth: there is suffering.**

In Pali, the word is dukkha. It's usually translated as "suffering,"
but its meaning is broader than that — unease, dissatisfaction, a
sense that something isn't quite right. This includes obvious pain —
illness, grief, aging — but also subtler things: the flatness at the
end of a vacation, the anxiety that lingers after getting something
you wanted, the sense that something is missing even when, on paper,
you have everything.

This truth doesn't claim that life is only suffering. It claims that
suffering is an inseparable part of life, and that ignoring it doesn't
make it any less present.

**The second truth: suffering has a cause.**

The Buddha taught that suffering arises from craving — from grasping,
from wanting things to be other than they are. Not just desire in the
ordinary sense, but any impulse to hold onto what's pleasant and push
away what isn't, and also the wish to keep going, to be someone, to
persist in some particular form.

Suffering doesn't come from things themselves. It comes from our
relationship to them — from trying to hold onto what's already
changing.

**The third truth: suffering can end.**

This is the truth that offers hope, which is exactly why it's usually
the hardest to believe. If suffering arises from grasping, then
release from that grasping is possible. Not denial of pain, not
escape from it, but a real shift in how we relate to it.

The Buddha didn't teach this as distant theory. He taught it as
something he himself had experienced, under the Bodhi tree.

**The fourth truth: there is a path.**

This is the practical truth — [the Eightfold Path](/en/lessons/2026-08-07-the-eightfold-path/),
which has its own article. But the key point here is that release from suffering
doesn't happen on its own. It requires practice — in conduct, in
concentration, in understanding.

***

These four truths were never meant to stay an idea. They only start
to matter once you check them against your own experience — noticing
the moment unease shows up, and asking what's actually happening
there. This isn't philosophy about life. It's an invitation to look
at it closely.
